1. Research Objectives

Define what you are trying to learn.

Examples:

Understand how customers currently manage inventory
Identify major pain points
Understand purchasing behavior
Determine why customers abandon a workflow
Understand willingness to adopt a new solution
Output

Research objective

2. Target Customer / User

Define exactly who you're researching.

Include:

Customer type
User role
Industry
Company size
Geography
Experience level
Usage context

Example:

SME electronics retailers with 2–10 physical stores in Lagos.

3. Customer Segmentation

Divide customers into meaningful groups.

For example:

Segment	Description
Small retailer	1 store
Growing retailer	2–5 stores
Multi-store retailer	6+ stores
Distributor	Wholesale operations

This helps you avoid treating every customer as one homogeneous group.

4. Research Questions

These are the questions your research needs to answer.

For example:

Behavior

How do store owners currently track inventory?

Pain

What is the most difficult part of inventory management?

Frequency

How often do inventory discrepancies occur?

Motivation

Why do they invest time in inventory management?

Alternatives

What tools do they currently use?

Outcomes

What happens when inventory information is inaccurate?

5. Research Hypotheses

Before conducting research, document what you believe might be true.

Example:

We believe multi-store retailers struggle to maintain accurate inventory visibility because stock is tracked independently at each location.

Then research tries to validate, invalidate, or refine that assumption.

This prevents confirmation bias.

6. Research Methodology

Choose how you're going to collect evidence.

Qualitative
Interviews
Observation
Focus groups
Contextual inquiry
Diary studies
Quantitative
Surveys
Analytics
Transaction data
Product usage data
Secondary research
Industry reports
Competitor reviews
Customer reviews
Existing research

Often you combine them.

Qualitative → understand WHY

Quantitative → understand HOW MUCH / HOW OFTEN

7. Participant Recruitment

Determine who participates.

Define:

Number of participants
Selection criteria
Customer segments
Recruitment method
Screening criteria
Incentives

Example:

Interview 15 SME retailers operating 2–10 stores.

8. Interview / Survey Design

Create the research instrument.

For interviews:

Introduction
Background questions
Current workflow
Pain points
Behaviors
Workarounds
Motivations
Existing solutions
Closing questions

Avoid leading questions.

Instead of:

"Wouldn't automated inventory tracking save you time?"

Ask:

"Tell me about the last time you reconciled your inventory."

The second question gives you behavioral evidence rather than an opinion about a hypothetical feature.

9. Customer Interviews

Conduct one-on-one conversations to understand:

Experiences
Behaviors
Motivations
Frustrations
Needs
Context
Decision-making

A useful interview structure:

Context → Behavior → Problem → Impact → Existing solution → Desired outcome

10. Customer Observation

Don't only listen to what customers say.

Watch what they actually do.

For example:

You ask:

"How do you track stock?"

They say:

"We use our inventory system."

But during observation you discover:

POS → Excel → WhatsApp → Notebook → Inventory system

That's a valuable insight.

11. Survey Research

Use surveys when you need broader quantitative evidence.

Measure things such as:

Frequency
Preferences
Satisfaction
Importance
Usage
Demographics
Willingness to pay

Example:

How often do you experience inventory discrepancies?

Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Often / Very often

12. Behavioral / Product Data

Your customers' behavior can provide evidence independent of what they say.

Analyze:

Product analytics
Conversion
Drop-off
Retention
Feature usage
Search behavior
Support tickets
Transaction data
Session recordings

For an existing product, this is particularly powerful.

13. Customer Journey

Map the customer's experience.

For example:

Discover
   ↓
Evaluate
   ↓
Purchase
   ↓
Onboard
   ↓
Use
   ↓
Get Support
   ↓
Renew

At each stage identify:

Actions → Thoughts → Emotions → Pain points → Opportunities

14. Jobs-to-be-Done

Identify what the customer is ultimately trying to accomplish.

Template:

When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [desired outcome].

Example:

When I am managing multiple stores, I want to know my stock position across locations, so I can make better purchasing decisions.

15. Pain Points & Frustrations

Organize the problems you hear.

Functional

"It takes too long."

Emotional

"I don't trust the numbers."

Operational

"I have to ask each store manager."

Financial

"We lose sales when products aren't available."

16. Needs & Motivations

Separate what customers struggle with from what they need.

Pain

"I don't know which store has the product."

Need

"I need visibility across stores."

Motivation

"I want to avoid losing sales."

This distinction is extremely useful when moving toward solution discovery.

17. Behaviors & Workarounds

Document what customers do today to solve the problem.

Examples:

Excel spreadsheets
WhatsApp messages
Phone calls
Paper records
Manual reconciliation
Multiple software systems

Workarounds are particularly valuable because they reveal existing demand.

18. Customer Quotes & Evidence

Capture actual customer statements.

For example:

"I usually have to call each branch before I know where stock is."

Then classify the evidence:

Quote → Observation → Data → Insight

Don't confuse a customer's quote with your interpretation.

19. Insight Synthesis

Now analyze the research.

Look for:

Patterns
Recurring problems
Contradictions
Behavioral patterns
Common motivations
Segment differences
Unexpected findings

For example:

15 interviews

→ 11 mention stock visibility
→ 9 use spreadsheets
→ 8 manually call branches
→ 7 report stock discrepancies

You now have a stronger signal.

20. Opportunity Identification

Turn insights into potential opportunities.

Example:

Observation

Store owners frequently call branch managers to determine stock availability.

↓

Insight

Owners lack centralized real-time inventory visibility.

↓

Opportunity

Improve cross-store inventory visibility.

Notice we're not yet saying "build a dashboard."

That's important.

21. Research Findings & Recommendations

Finally, summarize the research.

A good research report could contain:

Executive Summary

What did we learn?

Target Customers

Who did we research?

Key Findings

What patterns emerged?

Customer Problems

What problems exist?

Jobs-to-be-Done

What are customers trying to accomplish?

Pain Points

What creates friction?

Behavioral Insights

What are customers actually doing?

Opportunities

Where could the product create value?

Evidence

What supports each conclusion?

Recommendations

What should the product team investigate next?
