1. Problem Summary

A concise description of the problem discovered.

Template:

[Target user] is struggling to [task/job] because [underlying problem], resulting in [impact].

2. Target User / Segment

Clearly identify who has the problem.

Include:

Primary user
Secondary users
Customer segment
User role
User context
Relevant characteristics

Example:

Primary user: Multi-store retail owner
Segment: SME retailers with 2–10 stores
Context: Managing inventory across multiple physical locations

3. User Need

State what the user needs to accomplish, without jumping to a solution.

Example:

Store owners need a reliable way to understand current stock levels across all locations.

Avoid:

"Store owners need an inventory dashboard."

The second one already assumes the solution.

4. Problem Statement

Create the formal statement of the problem.

Template:

[User] needs to [accomplish something] because [problem/root cause], which results in [impact].

Example:

Multi-store retail owners need a reliable way to maintain visibility of inventory across locations because stock movements are recorded inconsistently, resulting in stock discrepancies, stockouts and poor purchasing decisions.

5. Current State

Describe how things work today.

Document:

Current process
Existing workflow
Tools used
Manual activities
Handoffs
Delays
Dependencies
Workarounds

Example:

Store managers record sales separately and send inventory updates to the owner through spreadsheets and messaging applications.

6. Desired State

Describe what better looks like.

Importantly, don't necessarily specify the product solution.

Example:

Current state:

Owner receives inconsistent inventory information from individual stores.

Desired state:

Owner can reliably understand the current inventory position of every store when making purchasing decisions.

7. Root Cause

Identify the underlying cause rather than merely documenting the symptom.

Structure:

Symptom

Stockouts occur.

↓

Immediate cause

Inventory levels aren't updated consistently.

↓

Root cause

Store-level inventory movements are recorded through disconnected manual processes.

8. Problem Scope

Define what is inside and outside the problem.

In scope
Inventory visibility
Stock movement tracking
Store-level inventory accuracy
Out of scope
Supplier management
Customer loyalty
Accounting
Demand forecasting

This prevents scope creep later.

9. Problem Impact

Document why the problem matters.

Customer impact
Time wasted
Frustration
Poor decisions
Increased manual work
Business impact
Lost sales
Increased operational costs
Reduced retention
Lower profitability
Operational impact
Duplicate work
Errors
Delays
Poor visibility
10. Evidence

Attach evidence supporting the problem.

For example:

Evidence	Source	Finding
Customer interviews	12 store owners	9 reported inventory discrepancies
Support tickets	3 months	47 inventory-related complaints
Analytics	Product data	35% abandon inventory reconciliation
Observation	Store visits	Stock counted manually

This is what separates a real problem definition from someone's opinion.

11. Problem Frequency

Determine how frequently the problem occurs.

Examples:

Daily
Weekly
Monthly
Occasionally
Only under specific conditions

Example:

Inventory discrepancies are discovered during weekly physical stock counts.

12. Problem Severity

Determine how serious the problem is.

You could use:

Low → Medium → High → Critical

Or a numerical scale:

1–5

Example:

Severity: 4/5 — High

Because discrepancies can directly result in lost sales.

13. Problem Reach

Determine how many users experience the problem.

Example:

70% of interviewed multi-store retailers reported experiencing this problem.

This gives you:

Severity × Frequency × Reach

which becomes useful later during opportunity prioritization.

14. Constraints & Assumptions

Document what could affect the problem.

Constraints
Limited engineering capacity
Regulatory requirements
Existing infrastructure
Budget
Integration requirements
Assumptions
Users have access to smartphones
Store employees record transactions
Owners need multi-store visibility
15. Problem Hypothesis

Turn the problem into something that can be tested.

Template:

We believe [user] experiences [problem] because [cause], resulting in [impact].

Example:

We believe multi-store retailers experience inventory discrepancies because store-level stock movements are recorded through disconnected processes, resulting in inaccurate purchasing decisions and stockouts.

16. Success Criteria

Before defining the solution, establish what solving the problem should achieve.

For example:

Reduce inventory discrepancies by 40%.

Reduce manual reconciliation time by 50%.

Increase inventory accuracy to 95%.

Notice that these are outcomes, not features.

17. Problem Validation / Sign-off

Finally, determine whether the problem is sufficiently understood to move forward.

You should be able to answer:

Do we know who has the problem?
Do we understand the problem?
Do we know the root cause?
Do we have evidence?
Is the problem significant?
Is it frequent enough?
Is it strategically relevant?
Can we measure improvement?
Are key assumptions understood?

Then the problem can move into:

Opportunity Assessment → Prioritization → Solution Discovery
