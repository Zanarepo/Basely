Here's what each section is doing.

1. Opportunity Definition
Clearly describe the opportunity discovered during research.
Template:
We have an opportunity to help [customer] who struggles with [problem] by enabling them to [desired outcome].
Example:
We have an opportunity to help multi-store retailers maintain accurate inventory visibility across locations.

2. Problem Evidence
Ask:
How strong is the evidence that this problem actually exists?
Capture:
Interview evidence
Observation evidence
Survey results
Product analytics
Support tickets
Customer complaints
Existing workarounds
Frequency of occurrence
Output
Evidence Strength

3. Customer Impact
Determine how significantly the problem affects customers.
Assess:
Time lost
Money lost
Revenue impact
Productivity
Frustration
Risk
Customer experience
Operational complexity
Example:
Inventory discrepancies cause store owners to make incorrect purchasing decisions and spend several hours each week manually reconciling stock.

4. Business Impact
Now look at the problem from the company's perspective.
Potential impact:
Revenue
Retention
Acquisition
Conversion
Engagement
Cost reduction
Operational efficiency
Expansion
Competitive advantage
This answers:
If we solve this, why does it matter to the business?

5. Market Opportunity
Determine whether the opportunity exists beyond your interviewed customers.
Assess:
Market size
Target market
Market growth
Industry trends
Geographic opportunity
Customer segments
Adoption trends
Typical concepts:
TAM → SAM → SOM

6. Customer Demand
Determine whether customers actually care enough to act.
Look for:
Existing spending
Existing solutions
Workarounds
Feature requests
Search behavior
Customer complaints
Willingness to switch
Willingness to pay
Important distinction:
"That's annoying." ≠ "I will pay to solve it."

7. Opportunity Sizing
Estimate the potential value of solving the opportunity.
You can evaluate:
Customer opportunity
How many customers experience the problem?
Business opportunity
How much value could solving it generate?
Revenue opportunity
What potential revenue could it create?

8. Frequency & Severity
A useful opportunity matrix is:


Low Severity
High Severity

Low priority
Medium priority
High Frequency
Medium priority
High priority

A problem that happens every day and causes significant financial impact is generally more attractive than something that happens once a year.

9. Strategic Alignment
Ask:
Does solving this opportunity support our product strategy?
Evaluate against:
Product vision
Product strategy
Company goals
Target market
Core product
Competitive positioning
Current roadmap
Example:
Strategic fit: High
Because inventory accuracy is directly connected to the company's core inventory-management positioning.

12. Unmet Needs
Identify what customers still cannot accomplish effectively with existing solutions.
Example:
Existing tools allow retailers to record inventory but don't give them sufficient confidence that their recorded inventory matches physical stock.
This is often where the strongest opportunities emerge.

20. Opportunity Score
You can create a standardized scoring model.
For example:
Opportunity Score =
Customer Impact
+ Business Impact
+ Market Potential
+ Strategic Fit
+ Demand
+ Feasibility
− Risk
Score each from 1–5.
This makes prioritization more transparent.

23. Assessment Decision
Finally, make an explicit decision.
Each opportunity should receive one of:
🟢 Pursue
Strong evidence + high value.
🟡 Investigate
Potentially valuable but evidence is insufficient.
🔵 Experiment
Worth testing before committing significant resources.
🟠 Defer
Interesting but not strategically important right now.
🔴 Reject
Insufficient value, evidence, or strategic fit.
